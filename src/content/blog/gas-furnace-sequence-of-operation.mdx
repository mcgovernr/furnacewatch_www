Every gas furnace runs the same play, every cycle, in the same order. Techs learn the
sequence of operation early because it's the backbone of diagnosis: find the step where
the sequence stopped, and you've found the neighborhood of the fault.

What's less discussed is that most of the sequence is observable from *outside* the
cabinet — each step has a mechanical signature. That's worth walking through, because
it's how a dispatcher can know where a cycle died before anyone opens a panel.

## The sequence, step by step

**1. The heat call.** The thermostat closes the circuit between R and W. Nothing moves
yet — but this is the moment the furnace is *asked* to do something, and everything
after is the furnace answering. If you're watching the W wire, you have a timestamp.

**2. Inducer prepurge.** The control board starts the draft inducer to clear the heat
exchanger and establish combustion airflow. From outside: a small motor spins up and
runs alone for a short purge. This is usually the first mechanical evidence that the
furnace heard the call.

**3. Pressure switch proves the draft.** The inducer's draft pulls in a pressure switch,
telling the board it's safe to ignite. Nothing audible changes — but if the switch never
proves (blocked vent, failed switch, weak inducer), the sequence stalls right here: an
inducer running with nothing following it. That stall pattern is itself a diagnosis.

**4. Ignition.** Depending on the furnace: a hot-surface igniter warms up, or a spark
igniter fires. Then the gas valve opens.

**5. Flame proving.** The flame sensor must confirm flame within a short proving window,
or the board closes the gas valve and retries. This is the step behind one of the most
common no-heat patterns in the field: the flame *lights* — combustion is briefly there —
then drops out seconds later because a dirty or failed sensor never proved it. The board
retries a few times, then locks out. From outside, that reads as an unmistakable rhythm:
flame on, flame gone, again and again, then silence.

**6. Blower on.** After a heat-exchanger warm-up delay, the circulating blower starts —
the biggest mechanical event of the cycle, and the moment the house actually starts
getting heat.

**7. Steady run, then shutdown.** The call satisfies, the gas valve closes, the inducer
post-purges, and the blower runs off the remaining heat.

## Reading a failure as a missing step

Flip the sequence around and it becomes a diagnostic checklist:

- **Heat call with nothing after it** — the furnace never answered. Board, power, or
  safety chain.
- **Inducer runs, nothing follows** — the draft never proved. Pressure switch, venting,
  or a rollout/limit holding the sequence.
- **Inducer runs, no flame ever appears** — ignition failed. Most often the igniter.
- **Flame appears, drops within seconds, retries, lockout** — flame proving failed.
  Usually the flame sensor.
- **Cycle completes but repeats constantly** — short cycling. Airflow, limit, or
  oversizing territory.

Notice what makes this list work: you don't need to see inside the control board. The
*order and timing of observable events* — heat call, inducer, flame, blower — carries
the diagnosis.
