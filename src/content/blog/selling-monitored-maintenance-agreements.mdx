Most residential maintenance agreements look the same from the customer's side: a
tune-up or two, a filter, priority scheduling, maybe a discount on repairs. They're
worth selling — agreement customers are the base of every healthy service company —
but they have a quiet weakness: **between visits, the customer gets nothing they can
see.** The agreement renews on habit, and when a household trims its budget, an
invisible service is the first thing cut.

A monitored agreement fixes the visibility problem, because it changes what the
product *is*.

## What a monitored agreement actually sells

With a monitoring sensor on the furnace, your agreement stops being "two visits a
year and a promise" and becomes: **"we're watching your furnace, and if it fails at
5 AM, we'll know — usually before you do."**

That sentence does three things a standard agreement can't:

1. **It works every night.** The customer knows the service is active on every cold
   night of the winter, not just the two days a year your truck is in the driveway.
2. **It reverses the worst moment.** The defining bad experience of homeownership in
   a cold climate is waking up to a cold house and *calling* for help. A monitored
   agreement means the call comes from you — "your furnace failed overnight, here's
   what happened, we're already scheduling it." Nobody cancels the company that did
   that.
3. **It's a line the competitor can't say.** Any company can match your tune-up price.
   "We watch your furnace" is a different product, not a cheaper version of the same one.

## Structuring the tier

Keep your standard agreement, and put the monitored agreement above it as a premium
tier. A structure that works:

- **Standard**: what you sell today — tune-up, filter, priority scheduling.
- **Monitored**: everything in Standard, plus the sensor, monitoring, and the promise
  that a failure gets a call from your office with a diagnosis in hand.

Price the monitored tier at a margin over your per-furnace monitoring cost — you set
the retail number, and the difference is yours. The pitch writes itself against what
the customer already fears: one avoided emergency visit, or one cold morning that
didn't happen, is the whole year's upgrade justified in their head.

## The renewal conversation

The renewal is where monitoring earns its keep. A standard agreement renews with an
invoice. A monitored agreement renews with a story: what the furnace did this season,
what got caught, what the office saw. Even a season where nothing failed is a story —
"your furnace ran clean all winter, and we were watching the whole time" is exactly
what the customer paid for.

## Objections you'll hear

- **"My furnace is new."** New furnaces are the ones you warranted. Monitoring
  protects your installation as much as their comfort — and warranty-period failures
  are the ones you most want to know about first.
- **"Is this a smart-home gadget?"** No — it's not a thermostat and it doesn't need
  the homeowner to install or configure anything. Your technician installs it at the
  furnace during the maintenance visit, and it watches the equipment itself.
- **"What do I have to do?"** Nothing. That's the pitch.
